## Lektion 1: Strategy

**Q: What is strategy according to Porter?**

Strategy = deliberate choice of different activities, or performing similar activities differently, to create a unique value mix. Strategic positioning means being different — not just better or more efficient. Operational effectiveness is not strategy.

**Q: What assumptions underlie a strategy?**

Strategies rest on assumptions about the environment (competitors, customers, technology), the firm's capabilities, and what value looks like. These assumptions can be wrong. Strategic reflection means making those assumptions explicit and testing them.

## Lektion 2: Strategic Analysis

**Q: How would you analyse a firm's strategic situation?**

Use external analysis tools (e.g., Porter's Five Forces: industry rivalry, threat of entry, buyer power, supplier power, substitutes) to understand competitive dynamics. Combine with internal analysis (resources, capabilities, value chain) to identify strategic position and gaps.

**Q: What are typical strategic moves for an incumbent facing digital disruption?**

Options include: defend existing position, invest in new digital channels, acquire digital capabilities, partner with digital players, or disrupt yourself before others do. The choice depends on strategic fit with the business model.

## Lektion 3: Digital Business Models

**Q: What are the four digital business model types (Weill & Woerner)?**

1. **Omnichannel business** — physical + digital, moderate customer knowledge, controlled value chain (e.g., retail chains with apps)
2. **Supplier** — supplies to others, limited customer relationship
3. **Ecosystem driver** — hub of a broad ecosystem, high customer knowledge (e.g., Amazon, Alibaba)
4. **Modular producer** — plug-in capabilities for others' ecosystems (e.g., PayPal, Lobyco potentially)

**Q: What is a customer value proposition and why is it central?**

The CVP is what a firm offers customers that makes them choose it over alternatives. It's the anchor of the business model — all other elements (resources, channels, revenue model) should align with it. In digital business models, the CVP often extends beyond product to data, personalisation, and experience.

**Q: What is the Business Model Canvas and what are its limitations?**

BMC (Osterwalder & Pigneur 2010) maps 9 building blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships, cost structure. **Limitation:** It describes a business model at a point in time — it is not a strategy. It doesn't show competitive dynamics, industry forces, or strategic trade-offs.

## Lektion 4: Digital Transformation

**Q: What is the difference between Digital Transformation and IT-Enabled Organizational Transformation?**

(Wessel et al. 2021)

- **IT-Enabled Org. Transformation:** Digital technology supports and reinforces an *existing* value proposition and identity. The firm's identity doesn't change.
- **Digital Transformation:** Digital technology is *central to redefining* value propositions, leading to a *new organizational identity*.

Example: A hospital implementing EMR (IT-enabled) vs. a machine manufacturer whose software becomes the core product — identity changes from "hardware company" to "software/controls company" (digital transformation).

**Q: Is Coop undergoing digital transformation or IT-enabled transformation?**

Coop is doing **IT-enabled transformation**. The app supports and reinforces the existing store-based business model and member identity. Coop is not redefining itself as a digital company — evidence: closure of Coop.dk webshop and MAD confirms the firm's identity stays physical-retail-first.

**Q: Why does this distinction matter strategically?**

It prevents misaligned investment. If you believe Coop is doing digital transformation, you might argue it should build a full digital platform identity. If you correctly see it as IT-enabled transformation, you focus investment on where digital supports the existing value proposition.

## Lektion 5: Digital Innovation & Disruption

**Q: What is digital innovation?**

Carrying out new combinations of digital and physical components to produce novel products (Yoo et al. 2010). Key properties of digital technology enabling this: data homogeneity, editability, self-referential properties.

**Q: What is disruptive innovation (Christensen)?**

Disruption starts at the low end or in a neglected segment with a simpler, cheaper offering that incumbents dismiss. Over time the disruptive entrant improves and moves upmarket, eventually displacing incumbents who were focused on their best customers.

**Q: Is Coop App innovation or disruption?**

Coop App is **sustaining innovation** — it improves the existing value proposition (loyalty, store experience) without challenging the incumbent model. It is not disruptive. Possible disruptors to Coop: pure online grocery players (Nemlig), discount expansion (Lidl, Netto), or platform players offering food planning/ordering (if they ever reach scale).

**Q: What is recombination in digital innovation?**

Combining existing digital and physical components in new ways. Example: Coop App combines loyalty data (digital) with physical store visit (physical) via Scan & Betal and personalised offers.

## Lektion 6: Digital Ecosystems

**Q: What is a digital ecosystem?**

A network of interdependent actors (firms, customers, partners) that co-create and capture value together, often mediated by a platform. Characterised by: network effects, platform governance, value co-creation, data flows across actors.

**Q: What is the difference between a value chain and an ecosystem?**

Value chain: linear, controlled, sequential production. Firm owns/coordinates inputs → transformation → outputs. Ecosystem: networked, multiple independent actors, non-linear value creation, governed by platform rules rather than ownership.

**Q: How does Lobyco relate to ecosystem thinking?**

Lobyco as a **modular producer** can plug into other retailers' ecosystems (providing loyalty/payment infrastructure). This is a limited ecosystem play. For Coop to become an ecosystem driver, it would need to be the primary hub for Danish consumers' food planning — no current evidence supports this ambition.

## Lektion 7: Implementation of IT Strategies

**Q: Why do IT strategy implementations fail?**

Common reasons: gap between strategy formulation and execution, lack of capabilities or resources, underestimating change management, technical complexity and legacy debt, misaligned incentives, poor governance.

**Q: What is IT legacy debt and how does it affect strategy?**

IT legacy debt = accumulated complexity from historical technology choices that limits future flexibility. Bjørn-Andersen & Hedman (2016) documented that Coop had historical IT complexity that constrained strategic choices. Digital strategy must manage complexity — adding features adds debt unless governed carefully.

**Q: How does your hybrid governance stance relate to implementation?**

Recommendation 5 (pilots before scaling) is directly an implementation discipline. Hybrid governance demands that digital investments are tested before being committed at scale, which reduces the risk of accumulating costly initiatives that don't deliver.

## General "meta" questions

**Q: What can theories do and what can't they do?**

Theories provide analytical lenses — they help structure what to look for. They cannot prove empirical claims about specific firms. A framework like DVC or DBM tells you *what to analyse*, but the analysis must be grounded in evidence. The limits: theories are simplifications; real firms don't fit neatly in any one box; and frameworks developed in one context may not transfer perfectly.

**Q: How did you use theory in your paper?**

Selectively and applied — not as textbook descriptions. The logic was: theory concept → Coop observation → analytical significance → strategic implication. DVC and DBM were used because they pair naturally to answer *what value can the app create* (DVC) and *does it fit the business model* (DBM).
